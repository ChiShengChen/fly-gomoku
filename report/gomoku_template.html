Where the reward machinery would be

cells firing per move, by distance from the eye

population in the volumesampledrealshuffled

A fly learns by having dopamine rewrite the synapses between its Kenyon cells and their outputs. Across real moves, sampled Kenyon cells fired zero times. The reward machinery is not switched off here; the board never reaches it. The shuffled copy does light it up, which is the same finding as everywhere else on this page — scrambling removes the attenuation, so noise arrives where signal could not.

Two caveats kept in view rather than in a footnote. The medulla row reads n/a because those units are graded and never cross threshold, so a spike record cannot see them at all — a zero there would mean the opposite of what it looks like. And the punishment row rests on a single sampled cell, which is too few to carry any weight; the Kenyon-cell row, at 1,042 of 5,177, is the one that does.

So we gave it plasticity

appetitive conditioning, in the modality the mushroom body actually has

A learning rule bolted onto the board would multiply by the zero above. Olfaction is the input that does arrive, so the plastic version runs there: an odour is a combination of glomeruli, the reward is delivered by driving the reward-dopamine cells directly, and afterwards every Kenyon-cell output synapse that fired alongside dopamine is depressed. The bars are how much each odour's output dropped after training.

The result that needs no interpretation

The circuit, and the loop that costs it

The diagram is drawn from the connectome, not from a textbook figure: each arrow's thickness is the signed synapse total between two cell types and its colour is the sign, so an arrow that looks thin is thin in the animal. Read left to right and it is the path a move takes — photoreceptors, lamina, medulla, lobula, and the two descending neurons that in a real fly would move something.

The curves running backwards are the interesting part. L1 inhibits Mi1 across 120,383 synapses and Mi1 excites L1 back across 23,444 — a closed negative loop sitting on the second synapse of vision. An earlier run measured the signal losing about fivefold on the way to the lobula and named this loop as the cause, so the obvious thing to do was delete it.

It is not the cause. Cutting both return arrows makes L1 less responsive to light, not more — 4.11 % modulation down to 3.48 % — while deleting a random set of the same size and sign takes it to 3.81 %. Those edges are net drive into L1, not compression of it. Deleting every unmodulated input instead, so that L1 hears nothing but photoreceptors, buys 4.11 % → 4.66 %: dilution is not the cause either. What is left is the gain of the model itself, and the arithmetic is blunt — the photoreceptors swing 47 Hz of release across 59,050 synapses and move L1's membrane about one millivolt. The loop is real, drawn from its own synapse counts; the story that was told about it was wrong, and the way that was settled was by cutting it and measuring.

The clouds behind them

Each player has its own brain floating behind it — neurons at the coordinates the FlyWire volume gives them, out of 139,255. A cell lights on the move during which it fired, and fades over the next few. This is the same simulation that chose the move, not a separate visualisation: the stone that lands is read out of the cells you can see flickering.

Both clouds are the same neurons in the same places. Only the wiring between them differs, and the difference is visible as how much of the brain lights up at all.

About the flies

The bodies are real anatomy, not illustration: the mesh is NeuroMechFly (NeLy-EPFL, Apache-2.0, Lobato-Rios et al. 2022, Nature Methods), 65 parts assembled from the model's own SDF and reduced from 334,525 triangles to 81,866 so a browser can carry it. The legs hang in the model's neutral pose because they are placed by link pose without solving the joint chain — a posture, not a claim. The board, the reaching and the wingbeat are animation. The brains are the real thing; the scene around them is not, and that split is the whole reason this page exists.

What this is not

It is not a fly that learned gomoku. There is no learning anywhere in it — the connectome is fixed, there is no reward, no training and no policy. What runs is the wiring diagram with a board painted on its eye, and the move is read out of where the circuit is loudest. Any post claiming a fly was taught a game is describing a reinforcement learner that happens to be shaped like a connectome, and the way to tell the difference is on this page.

That claim is measured rather than asserted, and it fails for two independent reasons. The first is in the table above: the mushroom body sits about five synapses from the eye, the signal loses roughly fivefold at each one, and by the time it should arrive there is nothing left — across every move of every game, not one sampled Kenyon cell fired. The second would hold even if the signal did arrive: dopamine in a fly works by changing the strength of Kenyon-cell synapses, and the weights here are a fixed anatomical measurement with no rule that could change them. Reward would need a plasticity rule, a signal to drive it, and something to keep the input alive across five stages — three things this does not have, listed so nobody has to guess which of them is missing.

The fly cannot reach most of the board

The lobula columnar cells do not tile the visual field evenly, so their receptive-field centres land in only of the 81 squares. That is not a choice in the code — it is the sampling density of the real cell types. Moves to any other square are unreachable by this readout, and when the circuit produces nothing at all the move falls back to random, which is counted and reported rather than hidden.

The comparison that matters

Win rates over twelve games are mostly luck, so the stronger test is the one with a data point per board: show the real brain and the shuffled copy the identical position and ask whether they choose the same square. Alongside it, a plain peak-luminance heuristic — no neurons at all — plays the same boards, which asks whether the circuit contributes anything the image does not already say.

Where the honest version of this goes

A fly's visual system is not built for board games, and this project has measured what it is built for: LC4 and LPLC2 converge onto the giant fibre, 311 cells onto 2, and that pathway responds to looming at 280 Hz while beating its shuffle null. A demo that let the same circuit dodge an approaching object would be testing the thing the animal actually does. Gomoku tests whether a wiring diagram can be made to look like it is playing, which is a different and much easier question.
